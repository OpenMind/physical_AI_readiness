1 · Executive summary

Physical AI readiness is not an AI question. Every country that can plausibly compete has access to much the same models. They differ in electricity, actuators, magnets, machine tools, integration labour, and permission.

What we found

China's lead is large. It scores 82.9 against Japan's 69.6, the United States' 69.0, South Korea's 66.9 and Germany's 66.1. The margin has little to do with software or research, where America still leads. China simply holds first place in energy, in robotics and actuation, and in materials, all at once, while placing second in compute. The United States leads the two remaining supply-side pillars, compute and human capital; South Korea leads demand pull; no other country leads any. China installed 295,000 industrial robots in 2024 — 54% of the world total, and more than all other countries combined.[@ifr-world-robotics-2025]

Mechanical parts dominate the cost of a humanoid. A humanoid contains one to two thousand chips, but semiconductors account for only about a tenth of its bill of materials, and most are built on mature 28–90nm and 200mm lines where capacity is plentiful. Actuators are 40–60% of BOM; gearboxes are 30–50% of an actuator. The scarce components are strain-wave and cycloidal reducers, planetary roller screws, frameless torque motors, precision bearings and rare-earth magnets. Japan supplies the high-precision end of that supply chain; China supplies the volume.

Magnets matter. China refines 91% of the world's rare earths and makes 94% of sintered rare-earth permanent magnets; industry estimates put its share of heavy-rare-earth processing, which covers the dysprosium and terbium used in robot motors at 99% or above. Verified operating capacity outside China[@iea-rare-earth-elements-2026; @adamas-2026] is 12,000–15,000 tonnes a year against Chinese capacity above 300,000. The IEA expects about 18,000 t non-China to exist by 2035.[@iea-rare-earth-elements-2026] Since December 2025 three Chinese firms hold general export licences, defence end-uses excluded, and the extraterritorial October 2025 package with its 0.1% de minimis rule is suspended only until November 2026.[@mofcom-announcement-no-61-2025]

Battery cells and electricity are ample. A humanoid pack holds 2.3 kWh.[@figure-f-03-battery-2026] A hundred million of them would need 230 GWh of cells against some 3 TWh of installed global capacity. A million humanoids on three shifts would draw 2.8 TWh a year, under 0.01% of world generation. The constraint is not the robots but the inference and training infrastructure behind them, and above all the connection queue: more than 2,000 GW of projects are stalled in US interconnection queues, connection takes a median of over five years, and large power transformers carry lead times of up to four years.[@lbnl-queued-up-2026]

Most countries that can build physical AI are reluctant to deploy it. Three of the five most capable score below the median on operating latitude. Among the top five only China ranks in the panel's top tier on it. Germany reaches 66.1 on capability and 28 on operating latitude; Korea 66.9 and 41. China is the one place combining top-tier capability with genuine permissiveness, and the Gulf states pair near-total permissiveness with, so far, very little capability. This mismatch runs through everything else in the report.

Resistance moved from regulators to workers during 2026. Western AI regulation eased: the EU pushed its high-risk obligations for embedded AI back to August 2028, Australia dropped mandatory guardrails, and US state laws were delayed or narrowed one after another. Labour moved in the opposite direction. In July 2026, about 35,000 workers at Hyundai's Ulsan complex walked out over Boston Dynamics Atlas deployment, demanding a veto over installation, not compensation once the robots were in.[@ulsan-strike-2026] No jurisdiction has enacted a direct tax on robots. Deployment is now slowed mainly by the collective agreement, and in Germany by the works council's veto over monitoring-capable equipment.

Readiness is concentrated in specific metro areas. Beijing sets standards and publishes datasets, the Yangtze Delta assembles, the Pearl River Delta iterates, Wuhu builds low-cost units. In America, the Bay Area supplies models and capital but no factories, and Detroit integrates for a shrinking customer. Scored at cluster level, eight of the top ten are in East Asia.

The two indices

PAIRI, the OpenMind Physical AI Readiness Index, asks whether a place can build and run advanced embodied AI: energy and grid, compute and semiconductors, robotics and actuation, materials supply chain, human capital and software, demand pull.

OLI, the Operating Latitude Index, asks whether a place wants to deploy advanced embodied AI. We consider permissiveness of AI and machinery law, the strength of labour institutions, public sentiment, and demonstrated willingness to let autonomous machines operate. Higher means faster near-term deployment on both, so the maps read the same way. We keep the two indices apart because they rest on different evidence and because merging them would obscure the (generally) negative correlation.

2 · What the index measures

By physical AI we mean machines that perceive, decide and act in the world under learned rather than scripted control: humanoid and legged robots, general-purpose manipulators, autonomous mobile robots and vehicles, and the increasingly autonomous end of conventional industrial automation.

The question behind the index is a practical one. Suppose an operator wanted ten thousand capable general-purpose robots working in a given country within five years. What would have to be true of that country?

Electricity would have to be abundant, cheap, reliable and connectable. The robots themselves draw very little, but the factories that build them and the inference infrastructure that serves them draw a great deal, and in most rich countries the constraint is no longer generation. It is queue position, transformer lead times and substation capacity.

Compute would have to be available at two quite different scales. Frontier training is a natural monopoly of place, which is why about 75% of global AI supercomputer performance is in the United States and 15% in China.[@epoch-trends-in-ai-supercomputers-2026] Robot inference behaves in the opposite way. Latency, bandwidth cost, safety certification and data-residency law all push it onto the robot or into a building a few tens of kilometres away, so the compute map that matters here is not the training map. Underlying both is the embedded silicon layer, the automotive-grade MCUs and power devices and image sensors and encoders, which is largely European, Japanese and increasingly Chinese business.

A robotics and actuation base would have to exist already. This pillar carries the heaviest weight, 25%, because the evidence for it is the strongest and the spread across countries is the widest. It combines revealed integration capacity, meaning robot density and installation flow, with domestic OEM capability[@ifr-world-robotics-2025] and depth in the component supply chain that gates humanoid production.

The materials would have to be obtainable: magnets and rare earths, battery cells and their upstream chemicals, precision bearings and screws, and the machine tools that produce all of them.

People would have to be available in two kinds. Robot-learning researchers, of whom there are globally perhaps a few tens of thousands, and automation integration engineers, controls technicians, industrial electricians and maintenance staff, of whom there are nowhere near enough anywhere. These two stocks are diverging, and treating them as one is the most common flaw in AI-readiness indices.

Somebody would also have to want the robots. Demand pull is where demographics and wages come in. A shrinking prime-age workforce alongside high manufacturing wages makes the arithmetic work; a growing young workforce alongside low wages makes it fail.

Limitations and what the index leaves out

Regulation and public opinion stay out of the capability score. Model quality stays out since open weights make it a poor proxy for national advantage. Announced capacity is not counted as capacity. Gaps below about nine points between neighbouring countries carry no ordering information. Every input that is an analyst estimate rather than a citation is flagged in Sec. XII and in the dataset.

3 · Method, weights and data quality

3.1 Construction

Each of the 58 countries is scored on six pillars, each normalised to 0–100 and combined with fixed weights. Inside pillars, indicators are min–max normalised across the panel, winsorised where one country would otherwise flatten the scale, and log-transformed where the distribution spans orders of magnitude: electricity generation, robot installations, manufacturing value added, AI investment, developer counts, wages. Aggregation is a weighted arithmetic mean, with no compensatory penalties and no geometric term. The pillars substitute for one another at the margin, and anything more elaborate would imply a precision the inputs cannot carry.

Operating latitude is scored separately on four components of 25 points each. Law covers comprehensive AI legislation, machinery and product-liability regimes, and whether an applicable safety standard even exists. Labour covers union density and, much more importantly, sectoral union concentration in the industries that deploy first, along with works council rights and employment protection. Public covers survey-measured excitement, concern and institutional trust. Autonomy covers demonstrated willingness to let driverless and autonomous machines operate. In every case 25 means maximally permissive. The autonomy component is scored on civil deployment; where military and civil latitude diverge sharply the civil case governs, which is why Ukraine scores 5 of 25 despite operating more autonomous machines than any country on earth. Because it scores realized deployments rather than statute, it carries a trace of capability into the latitude index — only capable countries can demonstrate anything — and the effective-potential figure therefore counts capability on both sides of its multiplication. We accept the overlap because statute without deployment tells us little, but it is a known circularity, disclosed here.

Effective Deployment Potential is PAIRI × (0.45 + 0.55 × OLI/100). The coefficient encodes a judgement: reluctance delays deployment and raises its cost without preventing it, so a country with no operating latitude at all still keeps 45% of its capability score. That number is the most arguable one in the report, and both raw components ship in the dataset so anyone can substitute their own.

3.2 Why these weights

Robotics and actuation carries 25% because robot density spans two orders of magnitude across the panel and because component supply depth is visibly gating humanoid production right now. Human capital carries 20% because both of its sub-stocks are scarce and slow to build. Energy, compute and materials each carry 15%: all necessary, none currently binding for anyone in the top ten, and all more substitutable across borders than the robotics base. Demand pull carries 10% because it governs how fast adoption happens, not how much a country can supply.

We tested the obvious alternatives. Moving robotics from 25% to 15% and pushing the difference into compute and human capital lifts the United States past Japan into second and narrows China's lead over second place from 13.3 points to 10.9; the top five stay the same. Dropping demand pull and renormalising costs Italy 3.7 points, Korea 2.3, Germany 2.2 and Japan 1.1, and lifts India by 1.0. The ranking survives plausible reweighting, and since §3.4's Monte Carlo now resamples the weights on every draw, that robustness is measured rather than asserted. The margins between neighbours do not survive it.

The functional form was tested too. Recomputing the index as a weighted geometric mean — the standard non-compensatory alternative, under which a hole in any pillar drags the whole score — changes no ranking in the top group and widens China's lead over Japan from 13.3 to 14.7 points, because China has no weak pillar to be punished for. The compensatory choice, if anything, flatters everyone else.

3.3 Data quality

Inputs fall into three classes. Cited values come from a named source with a year: electricity generation and prices, IFR robot density where it is published, manufacturing value added, AI authors per capita, AI investment, working-age projections, wage levels, union density. Derived values are computed from those. And estimated values are analyst scores anchored to documented qualitative evidence, which covers component supply depth, robotics software capability, integration capacity, edge silicon tier, and robot density for the forty-odd countries IFR does not publish free.

About 55% of the weighted index rests on cited numbers and 45% on anchored analyst scores. We did not choose that split; the sources forced it. Nobody publishes a count of robot system integrators in any country. ICRA, IROS, CoRL and RSS publish no country breakdowns. Sensor market shares are behind Yole and TechInsights paywalls. IFR's full country tables cost upwards of a thousand euros. Country-level installed battery-cell capacity in gigawatt-hours exists only inside Benchmark and BNEF subscriptions. Section XII lists the gaps.

Four traps in the underlying data

China's robot density cannot be compared across years. IFR revised the Chinese manufacturing-employment denominator in World Robotics 2025. China reads 470 per 10,000 on the old basis and 166 on the new one, and the global average fell from 162 to 132 for the same reason.[@ifr-world-robotics-2025] The apparent collapse is a methodology change.

China is absent from LinkedIn-derived AI talent metrics. The AI Index authors-and-inventors panel excludes it; GitHub undercounts it because of the Great Firewall and domestic substitutes. A naive composite therefore zeroes out the world's second-largest AI workforce, so we apply a documented correction.

China's AI investment figure excludes state capital. The $12.4bn private number is set against an estimated $184bn of state guidance-fund capital[@aiindex-chapter-4-economy-2026] deployed between 2000 and 2023. The headline 23:1 US-to-China ratio is largely an artefact of that exclusion.

Announced capacity is not capacity. Across humanoids, magnets and battery cells, announcements exceed delivery by factors of five to fifty. Wherever possible the index scores what shipped.

3.4 How much the judgement calls could move the score

Roughly 45% of the weighted index is analyst judgement, and a score printed to one decimal place conceals that. To put a number on it we perturbed every analyst-assigned input and re-ran the whole index 2,000 times. Because one analyst assigned every score, the error model has to allow for correlated bias, not just noise: an analyst too generous to a country is too generous on all twelve of its scores at once, and independent perturbations would average that away. Each draw therefore shifts all of a country's analyst scores together by a single bias term (SD 6 points) and each score individually by idiosyncratic noise (SD 8 points), a total standard deviation of 10 per score — claiming we know them to about a tenth of the scale. Each draw also resamples the six pillar weights from a distribution centred on the published vector, so the results are not conditional on one hand-picked weighting. Estimated robot density, which stands in for IFR's paid table in 44 of the 58 countries, was perturbed lognormally with a standard deviation of 40%; the fourteen published values were held fixed. Operating-latitude components were moved by 3 points on their 25-point scales (again split into a shared country term and per-component noise), and the supply-chain layer count by 0.7 of a layer. Cited hard data — generation, prices, manufacturing value added, wages, AI investment, developer counts, demographics — was not perturbed at all.

The result is a 90% interval averaging 9.4 points wide, or roughly five points either side of the published score, and a median rank movement of about two places. That is the number readers should use when comparing countries: China first survives every draw and a separate 20% haircut on all Chinese-sourced inputs, while several published adjacent orderings do not survive at all. The full probability table, ordering by ordering, is in the accompanying methodology document and `pairi_uncertainty.csv`.

4 · The readiness map

China's 13.3-point margin over second place is the widest gap between any two adjacent countries in the table. Where it comes from is worth pinning down, because the usual explanation, that China has caught up on AI, is not what the data shows. China's human capital and software score of 73.8 is below America's 78.7, and its research impact per paper remains well behind. The lead is built elsewhere: 95.3 on materials against Japan's 80.1 and America's 48.5; 83.2 on robotics and actuation; 82.1 on energy, because of an electricity system 2.3 times the size of America's that added roughly 540 GW of capacity in 2025 against about 53 GW in the United States.

Four countries with almost nothing in common follow China. Japan (69.6) is a component and precision-mechanics power with a shrinking domestic market and no independent robot foundation model. The United States (69.0) leads compute at 91.6 and human capital at 78.7 by wide margins, but scores 48.5 on materials, barely half China's. South Korea (66.9) combines the world's highest robot density, 1,220 per 10,000 manufacturing workers, with the deepest single-group vertical integration anywhere and the strongest demand pull on the planet, and also with the most organised resistance. Germany (66.1) has the actuation, sensing and machine-tool base, and the lowest latitude score of any major economy.

Europe plus Taiwan and Singapore form a third group between 40 and 56, each strong in one or two pillars and thin elsewhere. Taiwan (55.4) is the indispensable second source for precision motion components. Switzerland (51.3) has the world's highest AI-talent density and elite precision mechanics on a very small base. Singapore (48.2) has extraordinary robot density and almost no friction, with no manufacturing depth behind it.

For everyone else the limit is capability, not permission — and India makes the point clearly. It scores 37.2 with the world's second-largest developer population, a fast-growing grid and $493bn of manufacturing value added. It also has a robot density around 10, manufacturing wages at about 6% of US levels, and a working-age population set to grow 19.5% by 2040. No arithmetic supports putting a $50,000 humanoid in front of a worker earning $3,960 a year. India will supply talent to the robot economy this decade without being much of a market for its output.

5 · Pillar analysis

5.1 Energy and grid

Leaders: China 82 · United States 68 · Qatar 64 · Saudi Arabia 62 · Norway 61

Most discussion of physical AI and energy addresses the wrong thing. A Figure 03-class humanoid carries a 2.3 kWh pack good for five hours at peak, an average draw around 460 W. Three shifts at 70% duty comes to 2.8 MWh a year per robot.[@figure-f-03-battery-2026] A million of them would need 2.8 TWh, which is 0.009% of world generation and less than Estonia produces. Robot fleets will not place a material load on any grid for at least a decade. What will is the inference infrastructure behind them.

That infrastructure is another matter. Data centres consumed 415 TWh in 2024, some 1.5% of global electricity, and are projected to reach 945 TWh by 2030, with accelerated servers responsible for about half the net increase.[@iea-energy-and-ai-2025] The United States accounts for some 240 TWh of that growth and China for 175 TWh, nearly 80% of the global total between them.

China's advantage here is large. Its electricity system is about 2.3 times the size of America's, roughly 10,580 TWh against 4,520 TWh in 2025 on Ember's compiled figures, which report demand of 10,573 TWh and 4,536 TWh respectively.[@ember-yearly-electricity-data-2026] The annual increment matters more: +491 TWh against +129 TWh for the United States and +18 TWh for the entire European Union, about twenty-seven times the EU's growth. On capacity, China's National Energy Administration reports total installed capacity reaching 3,890 GW at end-2025, an increase of roughly 540 GW, while the EIA records about 53 GW of US utility-scale additions[@eia-electricity-monthly-update-2026; @nea-national-energy-administration-2025], its largest year since 2002 and around a tenth of China's. Data-centre and electrified-manufacturing load is easily absorbed in China, at industrial tariffs between $0.06 and $0.10/kWh, and can be sited in Xinjiang or Inner Mongolia where valley rates drop to about $0.04.

The United States has the best energy economics of any large developed economy, 8.7 ¢/kWh industrial as of May 2026[@eia-electricity-monthly-update-2026], and a delivery mechanism that does not work. Some 1,312 GW of generation and 749 GW of storage are in active interconnection queues. Projects completed in 2025 took a median of more than five years from request to commercial operation. Of capacity that requested interconnection between 2000 and 2020, 13% was operating by the end of 2025 and 75% withdrew.[@lbnl-queued-up-2026] Another 549 GW holds signed interconnection agreements and has not started. Large power transformers now carry lead times of up to four years. NERC has raised its ten-year summer peak growth forecast by 69%[@nerc-long-term-reliability-2026] and flags thirteen of twenty-three assessment areas as elevated or high resource-adequacy risk within five years. Steel and queue position are the binding constraints — not fuel, and not cost.

Europe is the cautionary case, growing generation 0.7% in 2025 while paying about double US industrial prices. The United Kingdom pays the panel's highest industrial price at $0.32/kWh; Ireland pays $0.26 with data centres taking 24% of national electricity.[@iea-electricity-2026; @eurostat-nrg-pc-205-2025] Businesses in parts of the Netherlands face waits of up to ten years for a new grid connection. Germany pays $0.19/kWh with only 3.9% firm low-carbon generation. France at 79% nuclear and hydro and $0.125/kWh, Sweden at 68%, and Norway at 90% and $0.075/kWh stand out, and firm cheap low-carbon power is really Europe's one competitive energy asset for this sector.

Sorting the panel gives three groups. The US, Germany, the Netherlands, Ireland, Japan, Singapore and Taiwan are reliable but cost- or queue-constrained. China, India, the Gulf, Norway, Canada and Brazil have headroom. Nigeria at 32.8 firm outages a month, Bangladesh at 26.2, Pakistan at 22.0 and South Africa at 7.7[@worldbank-enterprise-surveys] are disqualified on reliability, and Ukraine has no usable post-2022 data at all.

5.2 Compute and semiconductors

Leaders: United States 92 · China 87 · Japan 83 · South Korea 77 · Taiwan 75

Training and inference concentrate according to different rules, and conflating them produces a badly wrong map for physical AI. Frontier training requires one campus, one power interconnect and one low-latency fabric, and does not depend on where the user is. Hence 75% of global AI supercomputer performance in the United States, 15% in China, and some 190 other countries splitting the last tenth; hence the Americas holding around 80% of data-centre capacity under construction. Installed AI compute worldwide reached about 15 million H100-equivalents by January 2026 and is doubling roughly every seven months.[@epoch-trends-in-ai-supercomputers-2026]

Robot inference inverts every one of those properties. A humanoid picking parts cannot wait 200 ms for a round trip to Virginia and cannot assume a WAN link stays up. Latency, bandwidth cost, safety certification and data-residency law push inference onto the machine or into a facility within tens of kilometres. Germany, Japan, Korea and China all look considerably stronger on that map than the training league table suggests, while Ireland, Norway and Singapore matter much less, since their capacity is export-oriented cloud rather than factory-adjacent inference.

Fabrication is where the geography is clearest. China holds about 10.1 million wafer starts per month on a 200mm-equivalent basis, 30% of a global 33.7 million. Taiwan has 5.8 million (17.2%), Korea 5.4 million (16.0%), Japan 4.7 million (13.9%), the Americas 3.2 million (9.5%), and Europe with the Middle East 2.7 million (8.0%).[@semi-world-fab-forecast-2026] Knometa expects China to become the largest single source of IC wafer capacity during 2026[@knometa-2026] while Europe slips to about 4.5%. Concentration at the leading edge is more extreme still, with Taiwan holding roughly 68% of ≤16/14nm foundry capacity in 2023, and Korea about 79% of HBM.[@counterpoint-2026]

None of which matters much for what goes inside a robot. A humanoid contains one to two thousand chips; semiconductors were about 10% of humanoid BOM in 2025; and the great majority are MCUs, motor drivers, power devices and sensor interfaces built on mature 200mm and 28–90nm lines, where capacity is growing and power semiconductors are the fastest-growing driver at +35%. Two things really are scarce. The high-end robot processor is close to a monopoly, with NVIDIA's Jetson Thor as the reference platform, fabricated in Taiwan, and no non-US, non-Chinese alternative at that performance point. Advanced packaging and HBM are tight.

The firms that supply robot silicon are mostly not the ones people name. Infineon holds 13.7% of automotive semiconductors, NXP 10.8%, STMicroelectronics 10.2%, Texas Instruments 8.5% and Renesas 6.7%[@techinsights-2026], which is half that market between them and predominantly European and Japanese. Japan supplies image sensors and encoders, Germany MEMS and grippers. China supplies about 83% of high-resolution automotive lidar units and 34% of SiC substrate.[@trendforce-2025; @techinsights-2026] Europe and Japan make the parts; China makes the robots.

Export controls split the world three ways, not two. A controlled-technology core of the US, Taiwan, Korea, Japan and the Netherlands produces the tools and the leading-edge silicon. An unrestricted ring covering most of Europe, Canada, Australia and India can buy what it can afford. A third, conditional-access category appeared in November 2025 when US Commerce authorised up to 35,000 GB300-equivalents to G42 in the UAE and HUMAIN in Saudi Arabia under security and reporting conditions[@commerce-department-of-commerce-2025], which amounts to sovereignty granted by revocable licence. It is also worth remembering that the AI Diffusion Rule formalising global tiering has never been repealed; it remains in force, suspended only by a contested non-enforcement posture. Unrestricted access is a conditional status.

5.3 Robotics and actuation

Leaders: China 83 · Japan 78 · South Korea 75 · Germany 72 · United States 59

Robot density measures thirty years of accumulated ability to specify, install, commission and maintain automation. In 2024 South Korea recorded 1,220 robots per 10,000 manufacturing employees, Singapore 818, Germany 449, Japan 446, Sweden 377, Denmark 329, Slovenia 315, the United States 307, Taiwan 302, Switzerland 294, Canada 241, China 166 on the revised basis, the United Kingdom 112 and Mexico 62, against a world average of 132.[@ifr-world-robotics-2025] That capability cannot be imported quickly, and no amount of capital substitutes for it.

Flow is the more urgent measure. Of 542,076 industrial robots installed worldwide in 2024, China took 295,000[@ifr-world-robotics-2025]: 54%, a record, and more than the rest of the world combined. Every other major installer shrank. Japan fell 4%, the United States 9%, Korea 3%, Germany 5%, Italy 16%, France 24% and the United Kingdom 35%. India grew 7%, to 9,100 units, about 3% of China's volume. Chinese domestic brands took 57% of China's own industrial robot market in 2024, up from 28% a decade earlier. Japanese robot orders meanwhile hit records, with JARA reporting Q1 2026 orders up 41.0% and production value up 22.9% to an all-time high[@jara-2026], but domestic shipments fell 5.3% by volume. Japan's industrial base is booming on export demand while its home market contracts.

The actuator question

Actuators account for 40–60% of a humanoid's bill of materials.[@mckinsey-scaling-the-humanoid-2026; @interact-joint-actuators-2025] Inside an actuator, the gearbox is 30–50%, the driver 15–20%, the motor 10–20%, mechanical parts 10–20% and sensors 5–10%. So somewhere between a fifth and a third of a humanoid's cost is in precision reducers and screws, and national competitiveness in humanoids runs through that fact.

The incumbents still supply the high-precision end. Harmonic Drive Systems of Japan retains an estimated 71–85% of the global strain-wave reducer market, depending on which trade research you use, since no audited figure exists. Nabtesco claims about 60% of medium and large industrial robot joints.[@nabtesco-2026] Germany and Japan supply an estimated 90% of specialised robotic ball screws.[@merics-2026] Even China's RV reducer localisation, above 80% of units, runs on machine tools that are roughly 90% imported and mostly Japanese, and foreign firms still hold about half of China's own encoder market.

The challengers supply the volume. Harmonic Drive's capacity grew 8% between 2022 and 2025 while Leaderdrive's grew 139% and Laifu's 150%. Leaderdrive posted 2025 revenue of RMB 570.7m, up 47%[@leaderdrive-2026], and is building toward a million-unit line. Nabtesco's Chinese plant runs at 110–115% utilisation. To put the incumbency in proportion, Harmonic Drive Systems' entire FY2026 revenue was ¥59.5bn[@harmonicdrive-harmonic-drive-2026], about $400m, which is less than a single humanoid funding round.

Component by component, the cost consequences are dramatic. Six-axis force and torque sensors once imported at ¥100,000 and up are now made by LinkTouch, which took over 70% of China's humanoid 6D sensor market in 2024 and shipped 70,000 units in H1 2025, about 95% of domestic shipments[@jamestown-new-gains-in-prc-2026]; Kunwei undercuts ATI by 30%. Tactile sensors have gone from over ¥100,000 imported to ¥199 domestic. Coreless cup motors costing ¥4,000 and up from Maxon or Faulhaber sell for ¥100 from Aoyi. LinkerBot's O6 dexterous hand goes for about ¥6,666, near $920, against roughly $140,000 for a Shadow Robot 24-DoF unit. Leadshine shipped over 120,000 frameless torque motors in 2025, more than twenty times the previous year. Unitree's average humanoid selling price fell from ¥593,000 in 2023 to ¥168,000 over January to September 2025, down 72% in under two years.

All of which produces the number that matters most in this report. McKinsey estimates that building a Tesla Optimus Gen 2 without Chinese suppliers[@mckinsey-scaling-the-humanoid-2026] would cost roughly three times as much, with the bill of materials rising from about $46,000 to about $131,000. That is not a mild sourcing preference but could sharply limit viable US cases in the US.

Announced against actual

Global humanoid shipments in 2025 are 13,300–18,000 units, with China around 90% of them; H1 2026 ran about 19,100 units with Chinese vendors at approximately 97%.[@smartanalytics-smart-analytics-2026; @omdia-2025] Set against that, China's MIIT projects output above 100,000 units in 2026 and has directed that more than 10,000 be in commercial use by year-end.[@miit-humanoid-robot-output-projections-2026; @miit-sasac-2026] AgiBot advertises 15,000/yr capacity and shipped about 8,400 in H1 2026. UBTech had capacity above 6,000/yr and delivered 1,079 units in 2025.[@ubtech-2026] Tesla targeted 10,000 Optimus units for 2025 and shipped about 150; Musk acknowledged in January 2026 that none were doing useful work, and Optimus has over 10,000 unique parts, none previously mass-produced. Figure went from one robot a day to one an hour at BotQ inside 120 days, which is real progress. Of Unitree's fleet, an estimated 250 units were doing genuine work in mid-2026, with research and education still around 70% of revenue.

Only a handful of non-Chinese buildouts look credible. Hyundai has committed over 25,000 Atlas units to its own plants[@hyundai-motor-company-2026], the largest humanoid deployment commitment on record, with Mobis building a 350,000-unit-per-year actuator facility in the US from 2028. 1X opened a vertically integrated 58,000 sq ft plant in Hayward[@1x-technologies-2026] targeting 10,000 units a year and scaling toward 100,000 by end-2027. NEURA raised up to $1.4bn in June 2026[@neura-2026], the largest European robotics round ever, with Amazon, NVIDIA, Qualcomm, Bosch, Schaeffler and the EIB participating. Schaeffler targets more than 10% of the humanoid component market by 2030.[@schaeffler-2026] None of them has shipped at scale yet.

5.4 Materials and supply chain

Leaders: China 95 · Japan 80 · Germany 58 · South Korea 56 · United States 48

A humanoid robot is, in material terms, an assembly of electric motors. Optimus Gen-2 carries about 28 body actuators plus some 50 in the hands. Nearly all of them use a sintered NdFeB permanent magnet, and high-torque-density designs need dysprosium and terbium to hold coercivity at motor operating temperatures.

China makes 94% of the world's sintered rare-earth permanent magnets and refines 91% of the world's rare earths, both on IEA 2024 figures[@iea-rare-earth-elements-2026], up from about 50% of magnets in 2005. Separate industry estimates put Chinese heavy-rare-earth processing capacity at 99% or above. China also holds about 69% of rare earth mining.

Batteries are not the constraint. A humanoid pack holds about 2.3 kWh. Global EV battery deployment in 2025 was 1.2 TWh against roughly 3 TWh of installed cell capacity[@iea-global-ev-outlook-2026; @sne-research-2026], so a hundred million humanoids would consume 230 GWh, under 8% of today's installed capacity and less than a quarter of what the United States alone expects by 2027. Cell supply will not become the binding constraint on humanoid robotics. What does matter in cells is qualification: power density, since robots discharge at high C-rate rather than a car's 0.3C; cycle life under thermal cycling; pack-level safety; and BMS maturity. Those competences are concentrated in a handful of Korean, Japanese and Chinese cell makers. LFP chemistry (which dominates stationary storage) is almost exclusively Chinese.

The asymmetry between the two is stark. Battery capacity is fungible and capital-intensive and can be built in four years, with roughly 700 GWh under construction in the US alone. Magnet capability is know-how-intensive, and China banned the export of rare earth extraction, separation and magnet-manufacturing technology in December 2023. Lack of process knowledge outside of China will limit efforts to develop alternative sources.

The control regime, and the date to watch

April 2025, in force. Case-by-case licensing for seven medium and heavy rare earths including samarium, gadolinium, terbium and dysprosium, plus their metals, oxides, alloys and permanent-magnet materials.

October 2025 (MOFCOM Notice 61), suspended until November 2026. Five more elements; processing and magnetic-materials manufacturing equipment; mining, smelting and separation technology; and a 0.1% de minimis extraterritorial rule under which any product made anywhere containing 0.1% or more by value of Chinese-origin controlled rare earths, or made using Chinese-origin rare earth technology, needs a Beijing licence. A parallel Decision No. 58 controlled lithium cells and artificial graphite anode materials on the same suspended timeline.

December 2025, in force. General licences granted to exactly three magnet makers, JL MAG, Zhongke San Huan and Ningbo Yunsheng, for pre-cleared civilian customers, with defence and aerospace explicitly excluded and hundreds of smaller producers uncovered. In January 2026 Beijing imposed Japan-specific controls covering rare earths, gallium, germanium, graphite and magnets.

Chinese magnet exports hit record levels in H1 2026. The United States was the only major destination receiving less than in H1 2024. Total rare earth export volume fell to 30,483 tonnes while export value rose 61.1% year on year.[@csis-rare-earth-export-restrictions-2026] Yttrium shipments to the US collapsed from 333 tonnes to 17 across the eight months after April 2025. For an index of physical AI, magnet access is determined not by price but by the bilateral relationship with China.

Announcements point to roughly 80,000 t/yr of ex-China NdFeB capacity targeted for 2030. The (more realistic) IEA expects about 18,000 t by 2035, under a fifth of projected non-China demand. Verified operating ex-China capacity today is 12,000–15,000 t across a dozen-odd plants; the largest: MP Materials' Independence plant at 1,000 t, e-VAC Sumter at 2,000 t with first commercial shipment in December 2025, Noveon at 2,000 t, USA Rare Earth ramping, Neo's Narva plant in Estonia at 2,000 t and Shin-Etsu Vietnam at about 2,200 t. MP's 10,000 t campus begins commissioning in 2028. Vulcan Elements' $1.4bn financing package supports a company currently producing about 10 t/yr. India's 6,000 t scheme opened technical bids in August 2026 with a two-year gestation. Meaningful non-China magnet supply therefore arrives between 2029 and 2032, if then, and the heavy-rare-earth grades robot motors require arrive after that. Lynas, the only scaled ex-China heavy separator, produced 8 tonnes of Dy and Tb in Q3 FY26.[@lynas-2026]

Only China has every stage of production: mining, separation, metal and alloy, magnets, cells, cathode and anode materials, precision mechanics. Japan is the strongest second, with magnets, alloy, cells, materials and world-class bearings, screws and harmonic drives, but it imports 63% of its rare earth metal from China and is now under targeted restriction. The United States has mining, is standing up separation and magnets, and has cells, but ranks 25th of 29 in machine-tool specialisation.[@gardner-world-machine-tool-survey-2025; @itif-mapping-industrial-strength-2025] Korea and Germany have cells, components and precision mechanics with essentially no magnet chain. Europe's cell-assembly countries, Hungary, Poland, Spain and Czechia, are single-stage economies. And Indonesia's projected 71% of 2030 nickel refining is roughly 75% Chinese-controlled, so geographic diversification is running well ahead of actual control.

5.5 Human capital and software

Leaders: United States 79 · China 74 · Germany 59 · Singapore 58 · Switzerland 55

Robot learning is a small field. The AI Index counts 220,520 AI authors and inventors in the United States, 50,460 in India and 48,520 in Germany[@aiindex-chapter-1-research-2026], but the population able to train a manipulation policy is orders of magnitude smaller. A Chinese industry study puts the number of people in China who simultaneously command multimodal model training, reinforcement learning and real robot control at fewer than 1,000.[@suntzu-china-robotics-talent-2026] If that is even directionally right, the global pool of true robot-learning engineers runs to the low tens of thousands, spread across perhaps fifteen cities.

Research output is concentrated in two countries. The United States produced 59 notable AI models in 2025 against China's 35, Korea's 8, and one or zero from everybody else, and took 46 of the top-100 most-cited AI papers of 2024 to China's 41.[@aiindex-chapter-1-research-2026] Robotics depth looks different: a 25-year humanoid-robotics corpus puts Japan first on volume with 1,486 papers, China second at 1,170 and the US third at 828 but far ahead on impact, at 44.2 citations per paper against China's 6.3. Japan's lead there is a legacy of the ASIMO era, and it has not turned into a single Japanese robot foundation model. Per capita the leaders change again, with Switzerland at 110.5 AI authors per 100,000 and Singapore at 109.5 both ahead of the United States at 64.8.

Most indices miss the distinction that matters here, which is that AI talent and automation-engineering talent are diverging. Germany ranks third in the world for AI authors and inventors. It also saw 49% of its electrician vacancies attract zero applicants in 2024–25[@kofa-2026], alongside 225,000 unfilled vocational technician roles and 45% of electrical-engineering posts with no candidates at all. The United States has the deepest AI talent base on earth and faces 2.1 million unfilled skilled-trades positions by 2030[@jll-us-skilled-trades-2026], with roughly 600,000 trades postings a year met by 150,000 apprenticeship entrants and a 5:2 retirement-to-replacement ratio.

China's pay data confirms the split. Embodied-AI algorithm engineers earn ¥720,000–1.3m with pay rising 10–40% a year; industrial robot system-integration engineers earn ¥350,000–650,000, rising 10%.[@suntzu-china-robotics-talent-2026] Employers are bidding hardest for the skill that is not the bottleneck. China's own estimate of its embodied-intelligence talent gap runs to about a million people.

Is software a differentiator? Partly, and less each quarter. The model layer is commoditising fast, with NVIDIA's GR00T and Hugging Face's LeRobot and SmolVLA explicitly open and cross-platform, and Chinese labs at ByteDance, Alibaba DAMO, BAAI and AgiBot shipping embodied models at a cadence one tracker described as one every 48 hours. Open weights let a Polish or Korean integrator start from a frontier policy without training one. Everything downstream resists commoditisation: the teleoperation fleets and factory-floor data that fine-tune a policy for a specific gripper on a specific line, safety certification, and the electrician who wires the cell. Software is a necessary but plentiful input, which is why this index weights integration capacity equally with software capability inside the pillar. Scoring software heavily overrates the US and Israel and underrates Korea, Japan, Taiwan and China.

5.6 Demand pull

Leaders: South Korea 88 · Germany 87 · Italy 84 · Japan 80 · Taiwan 78

We project working-age population to 2040 and use that to directly estimate humanoid demand. Taiwan falls 18.4%, Korea 18.2%, Italy 17.7%, Japan 15.2%, Germany 14.5%[@unwpp-world-population-prospects-2024], Romania 14.3%, Spain and Greece and Portugal 13.2%, and China 10.9%, which is a loss of 89.6 million prime-age workers. We set that against wages of about $4,380 a month in Korea, euro-area industrial labour at €40.3 an hour[@eurostat-hourly-labour-costs-2026], and about $2,240 a month in Japan. The robot-density leaderboard reflects the combination.

The opposite pattern appears in India at +19.5%, the Philippines +26.7%, Bangladesh +27.7%, Egypt +33.1%, Pakistan +44.3%, Kenya +49.7% and Nigeria +54.3%, against Indian manufacturing wages at roughly 6% of US levels and Indonesian pay around $200 a month. These countries will supply talent to the robot economy without being markets for it. Until recently, India added five million human GitHub developers a year. In stark contrast, India only installed 9,100 robots in 2024.

China is unique, being placed between the two. It's young enough to still have many human workers, ageing fast enough to experience the decline, wealthy enough (about $1,320 a month in manufacturing) for the economics to work, and is automating pre-emptively, financed by state support and capital.

6 · Operating latitude: law, labour and public opinion

6.1 The 2026 inflection

In 2026 regulatory pressure stopped rising in the West and started to fragment, while labour pressure sharpened. The two are now moving in opposite directions, with labour becoming the operative constraint. On the regulatory side, the EU's Digital Omnibus, confirmed by member states in May 2026, delayed Annex III high-risk obligations by one year (to December 2027) and Annex I obligations (covering AI embedded in regulated products) to August 2028.[@eu-2024-1689-2026] A humanoid or autonomous industrial machine is an Annex I product, so that is a twelve-month easing for embodied AI specifically. Australia dropped mandatory AI guardrails.[@australia-national-ai-plan-2025] In the United States, a December 2025 executive order set up a DOJ AI Litigation Task Force[@doj-ai-litigation-task-force-2026], reflecting "the policy of the United States to sustain and enhance the United States' global AI dominance through a minimally burdensome national policy framework for AI".[@eo-14365-2025] Colorado's SB 205 is being rewritten, New York's RAISE Act was amended toward transparency-only in March 2026, and Utah's law was narrowed twice. None of these laws targets physical robots; they target algorithmic decisions about people. American regulatory friction for embodied AI is close to zero.

6.2 The European compound constraint

A humanoid on a European factory floor has to satisfy the Machinery Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 from January 2027[@eu-2023-1230-2027], which for the first time imposes explicit requirements on AI-enabled machinery, self-evolving behaviour and cybersecurity, and moves certain AI safety functions into third-party conformity assessment rather than self-declaration. It has to satisfy the AI Act as an Annex I high-risk product from August 2028. Its maker faces strict liability under the revised Product Liability Directive (EU) 2024/2853 from December 2026[@eu-2024-2853], which treats software and machine-learned behaviour as a product and reverses the burden of proof in technically complex cases. Its cameras are subject to GDPR. In Germany, before any of that applies, those cameras engage §87(1)(6) BetrVG[@betrvg-betriebsverfassungsgesetz], the works council's consent right over technical devices capable of monitoring employee performance, where the sanction is an injunction and the right attaches at the planning stage under §90(1)(3), before the deployment decision is even final. Article 26(7) of the AI Act then requires informing worker representatives before any high-risk deployment.

When combined, these requirements mean a European humanoid deployment needs simultaneous clearance from a notified body, a product-liability insurer, a data protection officer and a works council: four veto points, sequenced, on different timetables. It is hard to see a path where European advanced physical AI prospers, except for defense and security applications, where these rules (presumably) do not apply.

The standards gap is sharper than the regulation gap

China's MIIT released a whole-of-industry humanoid and embodied-intelligence standards system in March 2026, built with over 120 institutions and firms[@miit-standards-system-2026], covering the full industrial chain across six areas including safety and ethics. The Western equivalent for legged and dynamically stable robots, ISO/CD 25785-1, closed its comment period in July 2026 with no publication date set[@iso-25785-1-2026], and the IEEE Humanoid Study Group's own September 2025 framework estimates 18 to 36 months to ratified standards.[@ieee-humanoid-2025] ISO 10218-1/-2:2025, published in March 2025[@iso-10218-1-2025], revised industrial robot safety and folded in the collaborative content of ISO/TS 15066, but it does not cover balancing robots. A Chinese humanoid maker has a national conformity pathway. A German one has a regulation (potentially) arriving in January 2027 and no harmonised standard to conform to.

6.3 Labour unions

Sector-specific union concentration appears to matter more than country-level union density. South Korea demonstrates this. Korean union density is 11.6%,[@oecd-ictwss] relatively low compared to the OECD Data Explorer average of over 30%. Korean public sentiment toward AI is the most favourable in Pew's 25-country sample, at 16% more concerned than excited, and Ipsos measures 69% excitement.[@ipsos-ai-monitor-2025; @pew-research-2025] But in July 2026 some 35,000 unionised workers at Hyundai's Ulsan complex, the world's largest auto plant, staged four-hour daily partial walkouts over the Boston Dynamics Atlas deployment, with more than 34,000 having authorised the action.[@ulsan-strike-2026] Their demand was consent rights before installation rather than compensation afterwards, on the principle that not a single robot enters the workplace without labour-management agreement.[@kmwu-korean-metal-workers-2026] Hyundai rejected employment guarantees tied to automation and finalised the Boston Dynamics takeover the same week. Kia's union had moved in May.

The United States has the same structure with different institutions. Overall union density is again relatively low at 10.0% and private-sector density 5.9%[@bls-bureau-of-labor-statistics-2025]. However, the ILA's January 2025 contract covering roughly[@ila-longshoremen-2025] 20,000 East and Gulf Coast dockworkers prohibits technology devoid of human interaction, requires one added job per semi-automated crane including retrofits, and routes any new-technology dispute to arbitration absent union agreement. Where American unions control critical points, at ports, in autos and in Las Vegas hospitality, they have won stronger automation vetoes than any European statute provides. Las Vegas Culinary Workers secured mandatory pre-implementation bargaining over any AI decision, and the NLRB ordered Amazon to bargain with the Teamsters in April 2026.

Germany's constraint is different. It is statutory and procedural, acting before decisions are made. Denmark, Sweden, Finland and Belgium combine union density between 50% and 68% with bargaining coverage above 80%, while Austria and France pair modest density with near-universal coverage.

6.4 Public opinion is becoming more permissive

Ipsos, Pew, Stanford and Edelman all find the same country pattern. Optimism is highest in Asia and emerging markets, lowest in the Anglosphere and Western Europe. Ipsos measures excitement about AI at 80% in Indonesia, 79% in Thailand, 77% in Malaysia and 69% in Korea, against 39% in the United States, 38% in Great Britain and 32% in Canada, the lowest in the sample.[@ipsos-ai-monitor-2025] Pew finds 50% of Americans and Italians more concerned than excited, against 16% in Korea. Edelman's 2026 Trust Index reads China 80 and the UAE 80 against Japan 38[@edelman-trust-barometer-2026], France 42, Germany 44 and the United States 47.

There are two nuances. Europeans are more positive about robots at work than about AI in general, with over 60% viewing workplace robots and AI positively in Eurobarometer[@eurobarometer-2025] even while distrusting AI broadly. Second, Western sentiment is improving, not deteriorating: global optimism rose from 55% to 59% in a year[@aiindex-chapter-9-public-opinion-2026], with Germany up 12 points and France up 10 since 2022.

Fiscal threats to physical AI remain hypothetical. No jurisdiction has enacted a direct tax on robots. The closest existing measure is indirect: South Korea cut its automation-investment tax deduction in 2017, from 3% to 1% for large firms, which was widely described at the time as the world's first robot tax. Proposals, including Senator Sanders' equity-payable AI tax (June 2026) and House Democrats' displaced-worker levy (August 2026), remain bills.

6.5 Permissiveness and underrated markets

Willingness to let autonomous machines run unsupervised is the best available revealed preference. China operates driverless L4 at scale across multiple city pilot zones, though Beijing paused robotaxi licensing after a Baidu fleet malfunction in Wuhan and only restarted in July 2026. The United States runs commercial driverless service in Arizona, Texas, California, Florida and Nevada, with Waymo's next five expansion cities all in permissive states. The FAA finalised Part 108 in April 2026, converting BVLOS drone operations from waiver-by-waiver to a standing framework.

The underrated markets are the UAE, Saudi Arabia and Singapore. Abu Dhabi granted WeRide a city-level L4 commercial permit in October 2025, announced that November, which removed the in-vehicle safety officer requirement. Since the human safety operator is what makes robotaxis unprofitable, that may be the most consequential single regulatory act for autonomy economics anywhere. Dubai followed with fully driverless fare-charging operations in 2026, and Riyadh permitted WeRide in October 2025. Switzerland issued Europe's first driverless passenger robotaxi permit in November 2025. Singapore pairs a purely voluntary AI governance framework with the highest government-trust score in the Stanford sample, at 81%.[@aiindex-chapter-9-public-opinion-2026]

The Gulf combines near-zero regulatory friction, no meaningful organised labour, top-tier public trust and state capital. What it lacks is capability, which makes it the natural first commercial deployment ground for hardware built elsewhere.

7 · Capability versus latitude

We plotted the two indices against each other. We observe that societies best equipped to build advanced physical AI are, with one exception, the ones least equipped to consent to deploying it. The engineering base is in China, Japan, the United States, Korea and Germany; three of those five score below the median on operating latitude, and only China combines top-tier capability with top-tier latitude.

That is not coincidence and is probably not about regulation. The same things that make a country good at building robots tend to make it slow to accept them. Deep industrial bases produce concentrated, organised workforces in exactly the sectors robots enter first. High wages create the economic pull for automation, and high wages are usually the product of the bargaining institutions that will resist it. Ageing populations create the demographic demand and also produce electorates with more to lose from labour-market disruption and more political influence.

China's exception is instructive because it is not deregulation. China is heavily regulated, with six or more binding AI instruments, mandatory content labelling since September 2025 and algorithm filing regimes. The difference is the regulation's intent. Chinese AI rules barely touch embodiment, and where the West has a standards gap China has a standards system. Permissive-plus-directive describes it better than permissive: the state does not only allow deployment, it specifies, standardises and finances it.

One further asymmetry has trade-policy consequences. Edelman found in 2026 that more than a third of respondents want fewer foreign companies operating domestically[@edelman-trust-barometer-2026], with the widest domestic-versus-foreign trust gaps in Canada at 31 points, Japan at 29 and Germany at 29. A Chinese humanoid entering a European or North American factory therefore carries a foreign-company trust penalty on top of an automation trust penalty, and that will sharpen as Chinese hardware becomes the only economically viable option.

7.1 Ukraine and iteration velocity

Ukraine scores 18.0 on readiness, fifty-first of fifty-eight, and is the case most likely to be raised against this index. Since 2022 it has gone from roughly ten drone manufacturers to more than five hundred, and its Brave1 defence-technology cluster now registers some 2,500 companies and over 5,000 products[@brave1-2026]. Output ran to about two million uncrewed systems in 2024 and roughly four million in 2025, against a 2026 government target above seven million[@mod-ua-drone-production-targets-for-2026]; the president has claimed ten million and independent estimates put the likely figure at five to six[@atlanticcouncil-drone-superpower-ukraine-2026]. Ground robots follow the same curve, with 25,000 unmanned ground vehicles procured in the first half of 2026, double the whole of 2025, and 16,676 recorded battlefield missions in June alone[@mod-ua-unmanned-ground-vehicle-procureme-2026]. Danish and American officials have said that Ukraine now builds these systems faster and cheaper than anyone in the West. Superficially, a country with a severely damaged grid, no semiconductor fabrication and no rare-earth processing has built a world-leading embodied-autonomy industry from nothing, which is precisely what an index weighted toward industrial depth says should be impossible.

Look at what Ukraine actually built, though, and the case supports the opposite conclusion. It built assembly, integration and iteration. It did not build the supply chain, and on the evidence it cannot. Estimates of Chinese dependence among Ukrainian producers run from 89% to 97%[@fdd-china-is-a-key-factor-2025; @justsecurity-how-ukraine-became-a-drone-2026]. Thermal sensors are 90–95% Chinese and there is no Ukrainian alternative[@armsmonitor-fpv-drone-localization-2026]; video transmitters and motors are close to wholly dependent; and the neodymium magnets inside those motors come from the same Chinese monopoly this report identifies as the tightest constraint in the whole supply chain. Ukrainian industry bodies claim localisation has moved from essentially 100% Chinese content in 2022 to about 38% today, a figure worth discounting for the interest of the party reporting it, and one that in any case describes the low-value tier. Ukraine now makes its own frames, propellers, batteries, antennas and boards. The single most telling detail in the record is that Ukrainian carbon airframes undercut Chinese ones on price because of better material efficiency — and the carbon itself is imported from China. When Beijing tightened export controls, component prices tripled[@fdd-china-is-a-key-factor-2025]. Ukraine's own answer, a domestic chip facility targeting 50,000 units a month by 2030 at a cost of one to three billion dollars, is negligible against a requirement measured in millions of airframes a year.

The autonomy content is also thinner than the coverage implies, which matters for an index of physical AI rather than of uncrewed systems. CSIS puts the number of AI-enhanced drones Ukraine bought in 2024 at around 10,000 out of some two million built, roughly half of one percent[@csis-ai-enabled-autonomous-warfare]. What is genuinely fielded in 2026 is terminal guidance and last-kilometre lock-on, plus optical navigation under GPS denial, running mostly on NVIDIA Jetson modules. Swarming remains small-scale experiment; autonomous target selection is outside protocol. But the effect size where autonomy is fielded is the reason to expect that fraction to climb steeply: terminal autonomy lifts strike success from 10–20% to 70–80%[@csis-ai-enabled-autonomous-warfare], a three- to fourfold improvement for a few hundred dollars of compute.

What Ukraine does demonstrate, and what this index does not measure, is that iteration velocity is separable from component sovereignty. A fifteen-second video from a front-line unit can produce a firmware change in days, and 413 new drone systems were codified in 2026 alone[@atlanticcouncil-drone-superpower-ukraine-2026], against European certification timelines measured in years. That loop is faster than anything in this report, including Shenzhen's one- to two-week board respin, because Shenzhen optimises for cost at volume and has no front line to learn from. The two capabilities are independent variables, and a country can hold one while scoring near zero on the other. Ukraine has world-leading velocity and almost no sovereignty. China has the inverse. The United States and Europe currently have neither, which is why the July 2026 US–Ukraine statement of intent is structured around testing and evaluation rather than purchase[@us-ua-statement-of-intent-on-drone-coope-2026]: what is being acquired is access to a learning loop, and 22,320 systems ordered under the American drone-dominance initiative had yielded fewer than 3,000 shipped[@cfr-reversed-russia-s-momentum-2026].

Three qualifications keep this from being a growth story. Ukraine's constraint is money. Foreign direct investment into its defence sector was $105m in 2025, Brave1 disbursed $68.5m across the year with a largest single grant under $190,000[@pravda-can-ukraine-produce-more-weapons-2026], and the industry ran an estimated $17bn below capacity while plants were idle. Its talent base contracted rather than expanded, with IT exports at $6.66bn in 2025, still below the 2021 level, after more than 120,000 technical professionals emigrated[@devua-ukrainian-it-services-export-2026]. The demand driving all of it is existential and military, which is a condition no other country in this panel shares.

So Ukraine belongs in this report as a boundary case rather than a counterexample. It corroborates the supply-chain finding from an independent direction, under extreme demand pull and with every incentive to substitute away from Chinese components, and it still could not. It also marks the clearest limit of what a six-pillar industrial index captures. Readers should treat Ukraine's score as an estimate of sustained general-purpose industrial capacity under peacetime conditions, which is what the index was built to measure, and not as an assessment of its ability to field uncrewed systems in a war, at which it is currently better than anyone.

8 · Effective deployment potential

Weighting capability by operating latitude helps prioritize where to put a fleet in the next five years. China's lead widens to 70.1 effective against Japan's 54.6. It converts nearly all of its capability into deployable capacity, and nothing else on the map does.

Germany shows the largest single reduction: fifth on capability at 66.1, eighth on effective potential at 39.9, a loss of 26 points and the biggest absolute discount in the panel. Germany can build robots. The difficulty is that a German plant manager who installs a sensor-equipped humanoid without a works agreement can be ordered to switch it off. The works council's right attaches before the purchase order is signed. Korea loses nearly as much, from 66.9 to 45.2, not through statutory process but through a concentrated union in the exact sector that would deploy first.

Taiwan, Singapore and Switzerland all outperform their raw scores, and the Gulf appears in the top twenty despite modest capability. The UAE, at 27.3 capability and 87 on operating latitude, produces 25.3 effective, which is level with Finland on half the capability and nearly three times the operating latitude.

For an operator, the practical implication is that the first ten thousand commercially deployed general-purpose robots outside China are more likely to run in Singapore, Abu Dhabi, Taiwan, Texas and Japan than in Germany, Sweden or Italy. Not because the technology is unavailable in Europe, but because the clearance path is shorter elsewhere.

9 · The 22 metro clusters

9.1 Why clusters and not countries

National statistics are the wrong resolution for physical AI, and the evidence shows why in a few concrete ways.

National figures average away proximity, which is an advantage. China installed 295,000 industrial robots in 2024, which tells you nothing operationally useful. What matters is that within about ten kilometres of Nanshan District in Shenzhen, more than 200 robotics firms are close enough to their suppliers that circuit-board iteration has compressed to one or two weeks. That compression is a distance effect. A robotics startup in Chengdu shares China's tariffs, currency and industrial policy, and gets none of the spatial compression benefits.

Hardware learning is also tacit and therefore local. Software knowledge travels in a git repository. Knowing why a harmonic reducer whines at a particular preload, or how to fixture a battery-pack weld so a humanoid can reach it, travels in a person walking between two buildings. Which is why Taichung's Golden Valley holds roughly 1,500 precision-machinery firms and over 300,000 supply-chain jobs in a 60 km corridor producing about $28bn a year[@taichung-city-2025]; why NEURA and TUM are jointly spending €17m on a single 2,300 m² RoboGym; and why Boston Dynamics is spending $100m to collapse three nearby sites into one 323,000 sq ft building[@bostondynamics-boston-dynamics-waltham-2026] explicitly to put manufacturing, AI, training and R&D under one roof. Firms free to distribute keep choosing, expensively, to concentrate.

Most decisively, the same country can contain clusters at opposite ends of the range, occupying different stages of the supply chain. Read as one country China appears uniform. Read as four clusters it is a supply chain with identifiable bottlenecks: Beijing does algorithms, standards and datasets, the Yangtze Delta does integration and volume assembly, the Pearl River Delta does rapid iteration and commercialisation, and Wuhu makes low-cost industrial units and holds the designation of China's first national-level robot industry cluster. The same applies in the United States, the Bay Area supplies models and capital but no factories, Austin builds through a contract manufacturer, and Detroit integrates for a customer whose orders fell 25%. A simple average such as "US robot installations fell 9% in 2024" lacks critical nuance.

Finally, the constraints that bind are local infrastructure rather than national policy. Eindhoven's council approved ASML's 20,000-job second campus while acknowledging the need for some 20,000 new homes by 2030. Pittsburgh's problem is the composition of its local investor base: local funds supplied $23m of the $1.5bn Pittsburgh startups raised in 2025[@cmu-robotics-innovation-center-2026], down from $140m a decade earlier, with ten exits. Baden-Württemberg's problem is the solvency of its automotive supplier tier, with Bosch alone announcing some 13,000 auto-parts job cuts in September 2025. Korea's problem is a union agreement. None of these is a national variable, and all of them determine whether robots get built.

10 · Country profiles

China — PAIRI 82.9 (1st) · Latitude 72 · Effective 70.1

China operates at scale at every stage of the physical AI supply chain, leading energy (82), robotics and actuation (83) and materials (95), and placing second in compute (87). Its weakest pillar relative to the others is human capital at 74, a figure depressed by the exclusion of China from Western talent datasets. Its strategic position rests on deliberately accumulated chokepoints: 94% of sintered NdFeB magnets, 91% of rare earth refining, 83% of high-resolution lidar, 54% of world robot installations, 97% of H1 2026 humanoid shipments. High latitude reflects directionality rather than deregulation, and the state supplies standards, capital and demand. The vulnerabilities are real but slow-moving: dependence on imported machine tools for its own reducer production, foreign dominance of its encoder market, an embodied-intelligence talent gap of about a million people, and a working-age population falling 10.9% by 2040.

Japan — PAIRI 69.6 (2nd) · Latitude 61 · Effective 54.6

Japan's position is the most commonly misread in the index. It takes second place on materials (80) and robotics and actuation (78), and fourth on demand pull (80), with Harmonic Drive holding an estimated 71–85% of global strain-wave reducers, Nabtesco about 60% of large robot joints, Sony in image sensors, and world-class encoders and bearings. Regulation is permissive: the 2025 AI Basic Act is promotion-oriented with no penalties, and a Level 4 autonomy framework has existed since 2023. METI is running a ¥1 trillion five-year sovereign robot-AI programme aiming at 10 million AI robots across 18 sectors by 2040. The gaps are equally clear: no independent robot foundation model, domestic robot demand falling 5.3% by volume while exports boom, 63% of rare earth metal imported from China against which Beijing imposed targeted controls in January 2026, and a working-age population down 15.2% by 2040.

United States — PAIRI 69.0 (3rd) · Latitude 48 · Effective 49.3

America leads compute (92) and human capital (79) by the widest margins in the index, with $285.9bn of private AI investment in 2025 against China's disclosed $12.4bn, 1,953 newly funded AI companies[@aiindex-chapter-4-economy-2026], about 75% of global AI supercomputer performance, and robotics VC at $18.8bn by mid-2026. Its materials score of 48 is fifth in the panel and roughly half China's. Federal regulatory friction for embodied AI is close to zero and still falling. Its constraints are physical and institutional, not technological: interconnection queues with five-year median timelines and 75% historical withdrawal, transformer lead times up to four years, 2.1 million unfilled skilled-trades positions by 2030, a 5% share of the global actuator market against China's 26%, and sectoral union vetoes at ports and in autos that are stronger than any European statute. Robot installations fell 9% in 2024.

South Korea — PAIRI 66.9 (4th) · Latitude 41 · Effective 45.2

Korea has the strongest demand pull on earth at 88, from a working-age population falling 18.2% by 2040 against manufacturing wages of about $4,380 a month, combined with the world's highest robot density at 1,220 per 10,000 and vertical integration nobody else matches. Hyundai controls the robot maker, the actuators, the batteries, the chips and the factories, and has committed more than 25,000 Atlas units to its own plants. Korea also holds 79% of global HBM, 16% of world wafer capacity, the highest AI patents per capita anywhere at 14.31 per 100,000[@wipo-world-intellectual-property-indicat-2025], and 260,000 NVIDIA GPUs committed nationally by 2030. It also has the July 2026 Ulsan strike, the first factory stoppage in automotive history over humanoid deployment, and the AI Framework Act, Asia's first comprehensive AI law, in force since January 2026.

Germany — PAIRI 66.1 (5th) · Latitude 28 · Effective 39.9

No country in the index shows a bigger gap between what it can build and what it will be permitted to deploy: fifth on capability, eighth on effective potential, a 26-point discount. The capability is not in doubt, with 449 robots per 10,000, Infineon at 13.7% of automotive semiconductors, Bosch and Festo and SCHUNK and Schmalz dominant in end effectors, Schaeffler and Ewellix in roller screws, world-class MEMS and encoders, NEURA's $1.4bn round, and demand pull of 87. Nor is the reluctance, which compounds: Machinery Regulation from January 2027 with no harmonised standard for legged robots, AI Act Annex I from 2028, strict product liability from December 2026, GDPR, and works council consent under §87(1)(6) BetrVG attaching at the planning stage with injunction as the sanction. Add 49% of electrician vacancies receiving zero applicants and an automotive supplier base in distress.

Taiwan — PAIRI 55.4 (6th) · Latitude 58 · Effective 42.6

Fifth on effective potential, ahead of Germany. Taiwan holds 17.2% of global wafer capacity and roughly 68% of leading-edge foundry, ASE takes 44.6% of top-10 OSAT revenue, robot density is 302, and installation growth was the fastest in the world at +33%. Taichung's precision-machinery cluster is the only non-Chinese base that could plausibly supply humanoid motion components at volume, and HIWIN launches planetary roller screws in 2026. NT$20bn is committed to a National Center for AI Robotics through 2029. Two things constrain it: a working-age population falling 18.4% by 2040, the steepest in the panel, and a deliberate choice to supply the finished product rather than own it.

Switzerland, Singapore and the Nordics

Switzerland (51.3 capability, 40.0 effective) has the world's highest AI-talent density at 110.5 authors per 100,000, elite precision mechanics with a machine-tool location quotient of 4.3, ABB and Stäubli, robot density of 294, and Europe's first driverless robotaxi permit from November 2025. Singapore (48.2, effective 42.4) pairs 818 robots per 10,000, the second highest on earth, with the widest operating latitude of any developed economy at 78, a voluntary AI framework and 81% government trust; land, power and the absence of manufacturing depth are what limit it. Sweden (50.6), Denmark (42.2), Finland (40.5) and Norway (38.6) all combine strong robot density, cheap firm power and excellent talent per capita with union densities of 49–68%, bargaining coverage above 80%, and the full set of EU regulations.

India — PAIRI 37.2 (22nd) · Latitude 55 · Effective 28.0

India is the country most often overestimated in physical AI, and the one it is most important to get right. Its assets are substantial: 21.9 million GitHub developers growing by five million a year[@github-octoverse-2025] and on track to overtake the United States by 2030, 2.55 million STEM graduates, 2,082 TWh of generation with capacity expanding 9.9%, $493bn of manufacturing value added, 34,333 GPUs empanelled under the IndiaAI Mission, and a ₹1.6 lakh crore semiconductor programme. Demand is the constraint. Robot density is around 10, installations were 9,100 in 2024 (about 3% of China's), manufacturing wages are roughly 6% of US levels, and the working-age population grows 19.5% to 2040. No arithmetic supports putting a $50,000 humanoid in front of a worker earning $3,960 a year. India will be a major supplier of software and engineering talent to this economy and a marginal market for its output this decade.

The Gulf — permissive, and not yet capable

The UAE (27.3 capability, operating latitude 87, the highest in the panel) and Saudi Arabia (27.5, latitude 83) score modestly on capability and top the latitude adjustment. They have the cheapest industrial power in the world at $0.036–0.11/kWh, conditional access to up to 35,000 GB300-equivalents under the November 2025 US authorisation, Stargate UAE's 5 GW pipeline and HUMAIN's 1.9 GW by 2030, Edelman trust scores level with China at 80, no meaningful organised labour, and the regulatory act that matters most for autonomy economics. What they lack is any domestic robotics, component or materials base. They are the natural first commercial deployment ground for hardware built elsewhere, and the natural buyer of whatever the capability leaders are willing to sell.

11 · What would change the ranking

Six developments could move the index materially. Each is given with the direction and rough size of its effect.

1. The Chinese export-control suspension expires in November 2026. MOFCOM Notice 61, including the 0.1% de minimis extraterritorial rule and controls on magnet manufacturing equipment, is suspended rather than repealed, and the suspension lapses in under three months. Full implementation would put up to $6.5 trillion a year of non-China economic activity at risk on the IEA's assessment. For this index it would take China's materials advantage from decisive to close to absolute and lower the effective scores of every country dependent on imported magnets, which is all of them. The thing to watch is whether the suspension is extended, narrowed or allowed to lapse.

2. Non-China magnet capacity beats either the IEA forecast or the announcements. The spread between about 18,000 t by 2035 and about 80,000 t by 2030 is the widest forecast disagreement in the analysis. If MP's 10X campus, Vulcan's Benson plant, e-VAC's Sumter expansion, Neo's Narva Phase 1B and India's 6,000 t scheme all deliver, US and European materials scores rise 15 to 25 points and the index compresses substantially. If they deliver on the historical announced-versus-actual pattern, nothing changes before 2030. Watch commissioning dates, and specifically heavy-rare-earth grades; financing announcements have not predicted delivery.

3. Western humanoid production crosses from pilot to line. Figure at one robot an hour, 1X's Hayward plant, Boston Dynamics' Waltham consolidation and Hyundai's 25,000-unit commitment are the four credible non-Chinese production paths. If any of them reaches five-figure annual output on a non-Chinese actuator supply chain, the 2.85× BOM penalty narrows and US, Korean and German robotics scores rise materially. Track shipped units and supply-chain provenance; nameplate capacity has not predicted output.

4. The EU Machinery Regulation applies in January 2027. That is five months away, and the ISO standard for legged and dynamically stable robots has no publication date. Either notified bodies develop workable interim conformity routes, or European humanoid deployment stalls on the absence of a procedure rather than any policy decision. ISO/CD 25785-1 progression and any Commission guidance on AI-enabled machinery conformity are the things to follow.

5. The Hyundai precedent generalises. The Ulsan strike established a template of prospective union consent rights over robot introduction, and the UAW and IG Metall are watching it. If prospective-consent clauses spread across automotive collective agreements in Korea, Germany and the United States, operating latitude scores in the three most capable Western manufacturing economies fall by five to ten points each. The terms of the Hyundai settlement will indicate which way this goes.

6. US grid delivery reform. America has the cheapest industrial power of any large developed economy and cannot connect it. FERC and state-level interconnection reform, transformer manufacturing capacity, and non-firm connection agreements, which the IEA estimates could unlock 750 to 900 GW globally, are the available measures. Success would lift the US energy pillar 10 to 15 points and make it the clear second-place country. Median queue times in the next LBNL Queued Up edition, and transformer lead times, are the measurable indicators.

12 · Limitations and known data gaps

These are set out so the index is not over-trusted.

The index measures the capability to build more than the capability to operate. More than half its weight sits on supplier-side depth — components, materials, wafer share, OEM capability — that an importer does not need. A capital-rich, permissive country buying open-weight models, licensed compute and imported integrators could deploy at scale from a low PAIRI score; the prose calls the Gulf the natural first commercial deployment ground while the table ranks the UAE 20th of 58, and that tension is real. The effective-potential figure inherits a builder's numerator, so it understates buy-and-operate paths, and open-weight robotics models are commoditising exactly the layer the capability pillars treat as a moat.

The materials weighting is conditional on humanoid-class actuation scaling. The BOM decompositions, the 2.85× figure and the magnet emphasis are keyed to humanoid bills of materials in a year when global humanoid shipments were under twenty thousand units and mostly pre-commercial. If deployment at scale is carried instead by mobile robots, arms and vehicles, which use magnets and precision reducers less intensively, the materials pillar overweights China's position.

Five dimensions are not measured at all, each with a predictable sign. Financing capacity and capital-market depth (understates the United States; our own Pittsburgh profile names investors as the binding constraint). Defence procurement demand (understates the United States, Israel, Korea and Ukraine; §7.1 shows what that demand can build). Immigration and talent mobility (understates open labour markets; flatters closed ones). Security-driven procurement restrictions (overstates the convertibility of China's export share; a DJI-style bifurcation would cap it). And the durability of latitude itself: statutory permissiveness and revocable ministerial permission are scored identically, though China's 72 is of the second kind, as the Wuhan robotaxi pause showed.

Robot density is unavailable for roughly 40 of the 58 countries. IFR publishes only 13 or 14 country values in its free releases and the full table is a paid report. Values for the rest are analyst estimates anchored to regional averages and industrial structure, and should be read as ordinal, not cardinal.

No country publishes a count of robot system integrators. Not IFR, A3, VDMA or JARA. Integration capacity, plausibly the most important single determinant of deployment speed, is proxied by robot density, installation flow and documented skilled-trades shortages.

Datacentre capacity is not comparable across regions. European figures from EUDCA and Pb7 use a 2024 baseline and a 50 kW+ inclusion threshold, while APAC and Americas figures are metro colocation inventory that excludes hyperscale self-build. Mixing them biases heavily against the US and China, so the compute pillar uses a scored composite instead of raw megawatts.

Country-level AI accelerator stock below the US, China and Korea is not resolvable. Epoch's dataset covers only 10–20% of global AI compute and about 2% of China's export-controlled chips, by its own caveat.

Sensor market shares are largely paywalled. CMOS image sensors, IMUs, force and torque sensors and encoders are available only from Yole and TechInsights. Lidar is the one segment with a clean public number.

STEM graduate absolutes are 2020-vintage and UNESCO field shares are mostly 2016–2019, which makes this the weakest evidence base in the human capital pillar.

OECD employment protection legislation values were not retrievable; the latest vintage appears to be 2019, and union density figures are largely 2018 ICTWSS. Labour scores lean correspondingly harder on documented sectoral cases.

Ukraine has no usable post-2022 energy data and is scored with heavy caveats. Its score should be read as sustained general-purpose industrial capacity under peacetime conditions, which is what the index measures, and not as a judgement on its uncrewed-systems industry, which leads the world on volume and iteration speed. Section 7.1 sets out why the two come apart, and why the index does not capture the difference.

The 0.55 coefficient in the effective-potential calculation is a judgement, not a measurement. Setting it to 0.35 would leave Germany at 49.5 and change the effective ranking order; setting it to 0.75 would drop Italy below Vietnam. Anyone who disagrees should recompute from the shipped dataset, which contains both raw components.

There was one rater. Every analyst-assigned score in this index was set by a single analyst, with no second rater and therefore no inter-rater reliability statistic. A different analyst working from the same evidence would produce different numbers, and §3.4 is our attempt to bound how different rather than a substitute for the missing second opinion. The publisher also has a commercial interest in this subject, which is stated on the final page.

Cluster scores are analyst-assigned throughout. They are built on the national pillar baseline and adjusted for documented cluster-level evidence, and no equivalent to IFR exists at metro resolution. Treat the cluster ranking as ordinal.

China is well evidenced where it matters here, and poorly evidenced where it does not. The three pillars that produce China's margin rest on physical quantities counted independently by bodies outside China: robot installations and density from the IFR, electricity generation and capacity from Ember and the National Energy Administration, magnet and rare-earth shares from the IEA and USGS, wafer capacity from SEMI, and the audited filings of its own listed suppliers. Those are among the best-evidenced inputs anywhere in the index.

The weakness is confined to one pillar. Human capital and software carries 20% of the weight, and it is where China is measured worst: LinkedIn-derived talent panels exclude the country, GitHub undercounts it, and the $12.4bn private AI investment figure omits state guidance-fund capital estimated at $184bn. We apply a documented correction to the talent and developer inputs, but residual uncertainty remains. Its direction matters more than its size: uncorrected, every one of those gaps understates China rather than flattering it. A reader who thinks our China score is wrong should expect it to be too low.

Two things could have the opposite effect. Chinese announcements routinely exceed delivery, so the index scores shipped units rather than stated capacity throughout. And China's robot density is not comparable across vintages after the IFR denominator revision, so the 2024 basis is used everywhere. Neither correction changes the ranking; both narrow the margin slightly.

Two countries are genuinely hard to score. Taiwan, because it is missing from some international statistical series, including World Bank manufacturing value added, so several of its inputs are national-statistics estimates. And Russia, because sanctions make its nominal industrial capacity largely unavailable for physical AI purposes; it scores 29.5 on capability, which probably overstates what is practically accessible.
